## The client

Fjordline Ferries is a Danish travel company whose website helps passengers find ferry information and plan bookings. Timetables are central to that experience: passengers need to see when services run, while editors need a dependable way to keep that information current.

For its 2023 launch, Fjordline Ferries engaged {{brand}} for development and support. The project used [Storyblok](https://www.storyblok.com) as the content management system and [Next.js](https://nextjs.org) for the website. The aim was to make schedule content easier to maintain without losing sight of the mobile experience.

## The challenge

A timetable is useful only when passengers can trust it. For Fjordline Ferries, publishing travel information involved more than placing text on a page: editors needed to make changes confidently, and passengers needed to find the relevant details without navigating through unrelated content.

The project focused on several connected problems:

- Timetable updates needed an editorial workflow that did not depend on a developer for routine changes.
- Schedule information needed a consistent structure so that pages remained readable as content changed.
- Passengers using phones needed clear access to travel information while considering a booking.
- The website needed a maintainable foundation for continued support after launch.

The challenge was not simply to give editors more fields. It was to make those fields meaningful, so an editor could understand what to change and how the published result would appear. At the same time, the front end needed to present information in a form suited to passengers rather than to the internal structure of the CMS.

## The approach

{{brand}} brought development and support together around the publishing workflow. Work was organised into phases that connected the needs of the editorial team with the experience passengers would see.

1. **Map the content.** The team identified the information needed on timetable and travel pages, separating reusable content from page-specific details.
2. **Build the editorial model.** Structured content in Storyblok gave editors defined places to maintain schedules and supporting copy.
3. **Develop the website.** The Next.js front end turned that content into pages designed for desktop and mobile use.
4. **Prepare for ongoing support.** The team considered how the site and its content model could be maintained after launch, rather than treating publishing as a finished handoff.

This separation gave the project a clear division of responsibility. Editors could work with travel content in the CMS, while developers could maintain the components and integration that present it. Changes to a schedule did not have to mean redesigning a page.

## The solution

The delivered website uses Storyblok to manage content and Next.js to render the passenger-facing experience. A structured model helps keep timetable information distinct from surrounding editorial copy, making it easier to locate the content that needs attention.

For editors, the practical benefit is a more direct route from a change to its published context. Instead of treating each timetable page as an isolated piece of markup, the team can work within a repeatable content structure. That structure also gives support staff a clearer basis for investigating publishing questions: they can look at the content, its fields and the component responsible for displaying it.

For passengers, consistency matters as much as the technology behind it. Familiar page patterns make it easier to scan travel information on a small screen, while supporting copy can provide context alongside the schedule. The project kept the booking journey in view without reducing the timetable to a sales element.

## What comes next

A timetable website needs attention beyond launch: schedules change, supporting information evolves and editors encounter new publishing questions. The support relationship gives Fjordline Ferries a route to address those needs while keeping the content model and website working together.

Future changes can be assessed against the same practical test that shaped the project: can an editor make the update clearly, and can a passenger understand the result? Keeping those questions connected helps protect both the publishing workflow and the travel experience.
